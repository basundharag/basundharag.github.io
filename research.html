Research

Understanding the Universe as we know it

Dark energy, gravity and the large-scale structure of the cosmos

I study the evolution of the Universe as we know it: how galaxies and the cosmic web grew out of tiny early fluctuations, and why the expansion of the Universe is accelerating today. Most of my work sits where theory meets observation, asking what large galaxy surveys actually measure and what those measurements can tell us about dark energy, dark matter and gravity itself.

Current projects

Are DESI's tracers telling the same story?

The Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) measures baryon acoustic oscillations (BAO), a standard ruler imprinted in the distribution of galaxies, using several kinds of tracers: bright galaxies, luminous red galaxies, emission-line galaxies, quasars and the Lyman-α forest. Its latest results hint that dark energy may evolve with time. We are testing whether the different tracers in DESI DR2 are self-consistent, that is, whether they favour the same expansion history when analysed separately. We use Gaussian Process reconstruction, which lets the data speak without assuming a particular dark energy model, and ask in particular how much the high-redshift quasar and Lyman-α data drive the result.

With Carlos Bengaly (Observatório Nacional, Brazil) and William Matthewson (KASI, South Korea). Builds on Ghosh & Bengaly 2024, which compared DESI and SDSS BAO measurements.

Gravitational waves from eccentric binaries

Pairs of black holes or neutron stars spiralling into each other emit gravitational waves, and detecting them relies on accurate template waveforms. Most templates assume circular orbits, but some binaries are expected to retain some eccentricity. We are developing analytic, ready-to-use Fourier-domain templates for compact binaries on moderately eccentric orbits that also include the effects of the objects' spins.

With Achamveedu Gopakumar (TIFR, Mumbai) and Hemantakumar Phurailatpam (Chinese University of Hong Kong), with a research associate at APU. Supported by an Azim Premji University small grant.

From cold dark matter to kination

What if some of the dark matter converted, in the early Universe, into a component whose energy is dominated by its motion, an era known as kination? Such a phase would change how the Universe expanded at early times, with possible observable consequences. We are studying how such a conversion could happen and what it would imply.

With Nirmal Raj, Darpan Chakrabarti and Ujjwal Upadhyay (IISc, Bengaluru).

Themes in my work so far

The Hubble tension and dark energy

Measurements of today's expansion rate from the nearby Universe disagree with values inferred from the early Universe. I have explored whether dynamical dark energy within modified gravity (Horndeski) theories can ease this tension, and whether BAO data from different surveys agree with each other. Tiwari, Ghosh & Jain 2024; Ghosh & Bengaly 2024

Galaxy number counts and relativistic effects

We see galaxies not where they are, but where their light makes them appear. Gravitational lensing, redshift-space distortions and other relativistic effects all shift what we count. My PhD work developed full-sky descriptions of these observables, studied how lensing alters galaxy counts, and checked whether observables change under conformal and disformal transformations of the metric. Tansella et al. 2018; Francfort, Ghosh & Durrer 2019; Ghosh, Francfort & Jain 2023

Testing gravity on cosmological scales

The Eg statistic combines lensing and galaxy clustering to test general relativity on cosmic scales. I worked on formulating it in terms of quantities surveys actually observe, and on modelling observables on smaller, non-linear scales. Ghosh & Durrer 2019; Jalilvand et al. 2020

Weak lensing and intrinsic alignments

Weak lensing measures the tiny, coherent distortions of galaxy shapes caused by intervening matter. But galaxies are also intrinsically aligned by the tidal fields they form in, which can mimic or mask the lensing signal. I have worked on shape and size correlations, higher-order lensing distortions (flexions), a unified alignment model for elliptical and disc galaxies, and whether lensing and alignments can break the distance-duality relation. Ghosh, Durrer & Schäfer 2021; Giesel, Ghosh & Schäfer 2022, 2023; Ghosh et al. 2024
